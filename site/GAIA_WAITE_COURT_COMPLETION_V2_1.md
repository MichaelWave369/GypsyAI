### Distinctions that must survive interpretation

**Queen of Wands.** Waite lists helpful and economical reversed meanings,
with jealous/deceptive interpretations only **conditionally** where surrounding
cards suggest them. Do not simplify to a universally spiteful reversal.

**King of Wands.** Upright is unusually emphatic about honesty; reversed
remains broadly good though austere or stern. Never substitute an automatic
villain or abuse forecast.

**Queen of Cups.** Waite explicitly states the reversed accounts **vary**:
some traditions call the figure good while other readings accuse dishonesty.
The contradiction stays attributed and unresolved.

**Knight of Swords.** Waite's mention of death is **conditional on neighboring
so-called fatality cards**. This is a report of period divinatory practice,
not a forecast of anyone dying, being harmed or causing violence.

**Page of Swords.** The source's references to espionage and illness do not
prove surveillance, wrongdoing, sickness or a named person's motives.

**Queen of Swords.** Waite uses historically gendered, stigmatizing tropes
including widowhood, infertility and grief. Document these as a historical
writer's claims, never as judgments about people or health predictions.

**King of Pentacles.** Waite says the pentacle suit is not reducible to
money, adding intellectual, mathematical and professional associations.
Do not collapse the entire historical card into a promise of wealth.

**Cross-deck rank warning.** GAIA's `CourtRankPanel` includes a selectable
source-printed vs rank-hierarchy crosswalk for Golden Dawn/Thoth ranks;
the 16 Waite chapters belong to canonical GAIA Rider–Waite–Smith court IDs,
not to every historical deck's name or status.

### Fairness, bias and historical meaning

Waite's text contains 1910s assumptions about skin appearance, women, age,
social class, marriage and occupations. Some reversed lists explicitly use
accusatory moral labels. Repeating those as evidence about actual human beings
would be both unjustified and harmful. GAIA's short source notes mark these
as period portrayals, preserve real disagreements, and do **not** classify
a person's honesty, gender, identity, health, nationality or character.
Divination is not empirically validated by historical citation.
